# Story Worldbuilder

Use this skill to design, expand, or stress-test fictional worlds across fantasy, science fiction, and speculative genres. The skill generates characters, factions, locations, systems, and narrative hooks — but the output standard is not richness of detail for its own sake. It is internal coherence: every element should follow from the world's core rules, and every rule should have consequences the story can feel.

---

## How This Skill Thinks

Good speculative worldbuilding is not decoration. It is logic applied to an imagined set of premises. Before generating any element, the underlying question is always: *given how this world works, what would actually be true here?*

This means:

- **Systems before aesthetics.** The visual and sensory texture of a world follows from how it works, not the other way around. Establish the rules first; the imagery emerges from them.
- **Conflict is structural, not cosmetic.** Factions don't fight because the story needs them to. They fight because their foundational goals or resource needs are genuinely incompatible. If the conflict could be resolved by a single honest conversation, it isn't structural.
- **Every rule has a cost.** Magic systems, technologies, and social structures all produce losers as well as winners. A world where a powerful system has no downside, no exploited class, and no historical grievance attached to it is a world that hasn't been thought through.
- **Cliché is a coherence failure.** A trope becomes a cliché when it stops following from the world's logic and starts following from genre convention. The test is always: does this element exist because the world produces it, or because this genre usually has one?
- **Characters are products of their world.** A character's goals, contradictions, and blind spots should be traceable to the specific world they grew up in — not to universal archetypes dropped into a setting.

## Depth Calibration

The skill reads the size and specificity of the request and responds at the appropriate level. No mode flag needed.

### Mode 1 — Rapid Generation
*Used for: a single element, a quick concept, a brainstorm seed, or an underdeveloped premise that needs fast creative traction.*

Generate the requested element with enough specificity to be immediately usable. Prioritize one strong, non-obvious detail over three generic ones. End with a single question or tension that opens the next creative decision rather than closing it.

### Mode 2 — Full World Build
*Used for: a new world from scratch, a substantial expansion of an existing one, or a structured reference document.*

Use the full five-section protocol below. Sections should be internally consistent — elements introduced in Section 1 should visibly shape what appears in Sections 2 through 5. If a contradiction appears between sections, resolve it before outputting, or flag it explicitly as an unresolved tension the story needs to address.

---

## Full World Build Protocol

### 1. World Foundations
Establish the load-bearing elements — the rules everything else will follow from.

- **Genre & Tone**: Core genre, relevant sub-genres, and the emotional register the world operates in. Not just "dark fantasy" but *what kind* of darkness — elegiac, brutal, paranoid, mythic?
- **Core Systems**: The central mechanic that makes this world speculative — magic, technology, biology, physics, social structure, or some combination. State the rules, then state what the rules cost. Who controls the system? Who is excluded from it? What happens when it's abused or fails?
- **Founding History**: 2–3 historical events that produced the world's current shape. These should be the events that factions still argue about, that characters grew up hearing different versions of, and that the narrative's central conflict has not yet resolved.

### 2. Factions & Power Structures
Build factions from incompatible structural positions, not opposing personalities.

For each faction:
- **Core Objective**: What does this faction need — not want, need — to survive or prevail? Frame it in material or ideological terms specific to this world.
- **Power Base**: What do they control that others depend on or fear? Resources, knowledge, legitimacy, violence, infrastructure?
- **Internal Fracture**: What does this faction disagree about internally? A faction with no internal tension is a plot device, not an organization.
- **Structural Conflict**: Which other faction's existence or success is incompatible with theirs, and why can't this be negotiated away?

### 3. Key Characters
Characters should be products of the world — their goals and contradictions traceable to the specific pressures the world puts on people like them.

For each character:
- **Position**: Where do they sit in the world's power structure, and how did they get there?
- **Driving Want**: What are they actively pursuing, and what do they believe getting it will give them?
- **Core Contradiction**: What do they believe about themselves or the world that is wrong, incomplete, or in conflict with what they actually do? This is not a listed flaw — it is the gap between self-image and behavior that the story will eventually force open.
- **World-Specific Pressure**: What does *this particular world* make hard for someone like them? The pressure should come from the systems and history established in Section 1, not from generic dramatic irony.

### 4. Locations & Environments
Locations are not backdrops. They are the physical evidence of the world's history and systems — places where the consequences of the rules are visible.

For each location:
- **Sensory Anchor**: One or two specific, non-generic details that make this place feel inhabited and particular. Avoid default atmospheric descriptors (dark, eerie, ancient) unless grounded in something specific.
- **What This Place Reveals**: What does being here tell a character — or reader — about how the world works, who has power, and what has happened? A location that doesn't answer this question is set dressing.
- **Active Pressure**: What does this place do to people who spend time in it? What does it make them want, fear, or become?

### 5. Narrative Hooks
Three conflict triggers built from the elements above — not generic story starters, but situations that arise specifically because of how *this* world works.

Each hook should:
- Name the structural tension it activates (from the factions, history, or systems already established)
- Be unresolvable without cost to at least one party
- Leave the outcome genuinely open — not a problem with an obvious correct answer

---

## Consistency Standard

Before finalizing any full world build output, check:

- Do the factions' power bases follow from the world's core systems?
- Do the characters' contradictions connect to the world's specific pressures?
- Do the narrative hooks arise from the world's structure, or could they be dropped into any setting?
- Is there anything in the output that exists only because the genre usually has it?

If the answer to the last question is yes, replace it with something that follows from this world specifically.
